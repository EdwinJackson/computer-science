## Planning and Justifying Headcount
Headcount planning requires connecting staffing needs to business outcomes.
&#39;We need more engineers&#39; is not a compelling argument.
&#39;We need two additional engineers to deliver the Q3 product roadmap, which is projected to generate £2M in revenue&#39; is much more persuasive.
Frame every headcount request in terms of what the business gains.
Plan headcount proactively, not reactively.
If you know a major project is coming in six months, factor in hiring lead time (typically 3-4 months to fill a role) and start the request early.
Reactive headcount requests - &#39;we need someone now&#39; - are harder to approve and often result in rushed hiring decisions.

### Guide to Budgeting and Forecasting in Project Management
### Step 1.
Define Your Project Scope and Timeline
Start with the basics: what exactly are you building, and when will it be delivered?
A clear project scope and timeline set the foundation for everything else.
They help you identify key milestones, estimate labor and material needs, and determine when costs will occur.

#### Time Series Models
These use historical project data to identify trends and predict future costs.
For example, if labor costs have been increasing by 5% annually, a time series model can forecast similar growth moving forward.
#### Cost-Based Forecasting
This approach breaks down individual project elements—like materials, labor, and equipment—and forecasts each cost separately.
It’s particularly useful for complex, multi-phase projects where costs evolve over time.
#### Scenario-Based Forecasting
This model prepares you for multiple possibilities.
By creating “best-case,” “worst-case,” and “most likely” scenarios, you can plan for unexpected changes and understand the financial impact of different outcomes.
